What did fixing my own cars actually save?

I logged every repair I did on two used cars: the receipt for the parts, and what a shop would have charged. This page answers how much DIY saved, which repairs were worth it, and how far the answer depends on the mechanic estimates.

The data

What one row is

One repair on one car: what I paid for parts, what a shop would have charged for the same job (parts + labour), where the parts came from, which system of the car it touched, and why it was done.

Where the numbers come from

DIY cost: my receipts, CAD.
Shop cost: dealership/shop quotes where I had one; otherwise the shop's posted price or book labour hours × shop rate plus the part. These are estimates, which is why the sensitivity test below exists.

Files: repairs.csv · vehicles.csv

Method

  • The CSVs are loaded into SQLite by analyze.py, which runs every query in analysis.sql and writes the result sets this page renders. No result on this page is typed in by hand.
  • Savings are a view (estimate − parts), not a stored column, so they can't fall out of sync with the inputs.
  • Group savings rates are dollar-weighted (SUM(savings) / SUM(estimate)). The simple mean of each repair's percentage is , lower because cheap jobs like wipers and batteries count as much as a hood.
  • The script asserts that every breakdown (by vehicle, system, reason, source) sums back to the overall total.

Findings

  1. The result holds up even if the estimates are inflated

    The mechanic figures are the weakest input. If every one of them were overstated by 50%, DIY would still have avoided of the cost (). The conclusion doesn't hinge on the estimates being exact.

  2. A few big jobs carry the total

    The two body-panel jobs (hood and bumper) are of all savings, and the top repairs make up half. "Average savings per repair" () hides that most repairs saved a few hundred dollars and two saved over a thousand.

  3. Percent and dollars rank repairs differently

    The sway bar link has the best savings rate () but ranks # of 23 in dollars. The hood ranks # by rate and # in dollars. If you're deciding which skill to learn, dollars matter more; if you're deciding whether a small job is worth doing, the rate does.

  4. Where DIY made little sense: batteries and wipers

    The battery saved () and wipers . Shops buy these near cost and charge little labour, so there's little margin to capture. Both were bought retail; a parts-store price would narrow the gap further.

  5. Used parts gave the best return on parts spend

    Junkyard parts returned dollars of savings per dollar spent, mostly the used hood. That comparison flatters the junkyard: the shop quote was for a new, painted panel. See limits.

What this data can't tell you

My time isn't in the data. I didn't log hours, so I can't compute an hourly "wage" for DIY. Instead, the break-even table asks the reverse question: at $25/hour, how long could each job take before a shop would have been the better deal? Wipers break even at under an hour; the hood at about 50 hours.
  • Estimates, not invoices. I didn't pay a shop for any of these jobs, so the shop side is a counterfactual. The sensitivity test above bounds this.
  • Not like-for-like everywhere. The hood is a used, unpainted panel vs. a new painted one; the $30 bumper repair isn't a body-shop finish. Those two rows overstate savings for an equal-quality result.
  • Tools aren't counted. Tools bought for these jobs aren't in the parts cost. They're reusable, but they're a real cost of the first few repairs.
  • No risk or warranty. A shop repair comes with a warranty; a DIY mistake costs a redo. The model treats both as zero.
  • Small sample. 23 repairs on two cars I chose to fix myself. This describes my repairs, not DIY in general.

If I extended this, I'd log hours and tool purchases per job so the comparison becomes "net savings per hour" instead of gross savings.
