📱 The iPhone: A Single Consistent Series (2007–2025)

Apple is the only maker with an unbroken, comparable annual product line since 2007 — making it the cleanest lens for real vs. nominal pricing. Watch the sticker-price jump in 2016, when U.S. carriers stopped subsidizing handsets inside two-year contracts.

The 2016 "subsidy cliff": the iPhone 6S (2015) listed at $199 with a contract; the iPhone 7 (2016) listed at $649 unsubsidized — a 226% nominal jump. In real terms it's still a 222% jump, meaning this wasn't inflation catching up — it was carriers no longer hiding ~$450 of hardware cost inside your monthly bill.

💡 Key Insights

1. The First Cell Phone Is Still the Most Expensive

The 1983 Motorola DynaTAC 8000X cost $3,995 — $12,948 in 2025 dollars. No phone since has come close in real terms; even the $1,980 Samsung Galaxy Fold (2019) only reaches about $2,500 real, roughly a fifth of the DynaTAC's inflation-adjusted price.

2. iPhone's Real Price Has Been Remarkably Flat

The original 2007 iPhone's $499 launch price equals $777 in 2025 dollars. Today's base iPhone 17 costs $799 — a real increase of just 2.8% across 18 years, despite the device becoming vastly more capable.

3. "Prices Went Up" in 2016 Wasn't Really Inflation

The jump from $199 (iPhone 6S, 2015) to $649 (iPhone 7, 2016) looks like an inflation shock, but CPI only rose ~1.3% that year. The real driver was the end of two-year carrier contracts — the phone's true cost had always been ~$650; it was previously hidden in monthly service fees.

4. Flagship Android Has Converged With — and Now Exceeds — iPhone Pricing

Early Galaxy S phones matched subsidized iPhone pricing ($199). By 2024, the Galaxy S24 Ultra listed at $1,299.99 (~$1,338 real) — now the priciest "normal" flagship in the dataset outside of foldables and 1980s-era hardware.
